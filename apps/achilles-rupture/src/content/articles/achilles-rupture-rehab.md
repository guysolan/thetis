**Achilles rupture rehabilitation** is not just “wait in a boot, then stretch.” The job is to keep the healing tendon from healing **too long** (elongation), while you rebuild the calf strength you need to walk, climb stairs, and eventually return to sport.

Re-rupture gets the headlines. With modern care it is uncommon — often around **3–5%**. The quieter problem that limits push-off power is **tendon elongation**: the tendon heals attached, but longer and slacker than before.

## Key takeaways

- **Re-rupture is uncommon** with modern protocols; **elongation** is the more common reason push-off feels weak.
- Protect **foot position** (boot, wedges, night protection) while loading hard in a **tiptoe (plantarflexed)** position under guidance.
- **Early weight-bearing in the boot**, as tolerated, is usually encouraged — uncontrolled toe-up motion is the risk.
- Boot removal is typically around **weeks 10–12**, when strength is ready for walking loads — not as soon as you “feel fine.”
- An early post-boot target is often **15 quality single-leg heel raises**; later sport readiness usually needs **25+** with good height and control.
- **Do not force calf stretches** early. Tightness usually eases with gradual loaded movement over many months.
- Return to sport should be **criteria-based** (strength, hopping, confidence), typically around **9 months** for many patients — not a date alone.

## What actually happens when the Achilles ruptures

The Achilles is the strongest tendon in the body. Ruptures often happen during a movement you have done a thousand times: a step-back, push-off, or change of direction. It does **not** always take a maximal jump or landing.

What they share is usually **fast movement into dorsiflexion** — the ankle bending so the toes move toward the shin — under load. Fatigue in the tissue over many strain episodes also plays a role. That is why it can feel like it “just went” during something ordinary.

After the tear, new tissue bridges the gap. That tissue starts soft and stretchy. If it is pulled long while it is still soft, you can end up with a tendon that is continuous but **too long** for powerful push-off.

## Re-rupture vs elongation: which should you worry about?

| Problem | What it is | How common | What patients notice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Re-rupture** | The tendon tears again | About **3–5%** with modern care | Sudden pop, sharp pain, loss of push-off |
| **Elongation** | Tendon heals longer / slacker | Common on imaging to some degree | Weak push-off, low heel raises, limp when tired |

Almost everyone gets **some** lengthening. A small amount can still work well. Too much, and the calf muscle sits shorter on a longer tendon — the whole unit can look “normal” on some measures while power suffers.

Elongation can happen at surgery, during the boot phase (poor position or removing the boot too soon), or after the boot if you mobilise too aggressively. That is why protocol adherence matters as much as which treatment you started with.

## Surgery vs non-surgical care

Both pathways can work well when rehab is good. Large trials of accelerated functional rehabilitation found **similar outcomes** for surgery and non-surgery when early weight-bearing and guided rehab were used, with surgery carrying more wound-related complications.

**Non-surgical care is first-line for most patients.** Surgery may still be chosen for specific reasons — for example a large gap, delayed presentation, or some high-demand athletes — after discussion with your surgeon.

The practical takeaway: obsess less about the initial ultrasound gap, and more about **doing the rehab well**. Rehab quality is often the bigger driver of how strong you feel months later.

See also [why surgery isn’t usually needed](/articles/why-surgery-isnt-usually-needed-for-achilles-tendon-ruptures).

## The two rules of early rehab

Think of early recovery as two jobs at once:

1. **Protect length** — keep the ankle in a tiptoe position in the boot (and overnight as advised) so the tendon ends stay close.
2. **Build strength safely** — load the calf in plantarflexion under physio guidance, rather than waiting passively for months.

Loading is not the enemy. **Uncontrolled dorsiflexion** (toes up too early, stretching hard, walking without the boot, sleeping without agreed night protection) is.

| Safe early idea | Risky early idea |
| --- | --- |
| Walking in the boot as your team advises | Taking the boot off to “try normal walking” |
| Seated tip-toe loading with guidance | Forcing the ankle flat or stretched |
| Following wedge / hinge schedule exactly | Removing wedges early because it feels easier |
| Night protection as prescribed | Sleeping without protection in the early weeks |

If sleeping in the boot is miserable, a purpose-built night position option such as the [Achilles rupture night splint](https://dub.sh/mmkrz0Z) is often discussed as an alternative — still follow your own clinician’s night rules.

## Boot phase: weeks that matter

Typical modern boot time is roughly **10–12 weeks**, with position gradually reduced on a schedule (wedges or a hinge). That is longer than some “accelerated” pathways that aim for boot-off around 8 weeks.

Leaving the boot early is tempting. Walking without enough calf-tendon strength puts large forces through tissue that is still soft — everyday walking can load the Achilles several times body weight. If the tendon is not ready, that is a classic elongation window.

During the boot phase, many programmes still start **protected loading** early (often from around weeks 2–3): seated plantarflexion work, foot muscle exercises, then progressing toward standing isometrics later in the boot period. Your physio sets the loads. Do not invent your own progression from social media.

Boot choice (Aircast vs VACOped) matters less than **wearing it correctly and sticking to the schedule**. Compare options in [Aircast vs VACOped](/articles/aircast-vs-vacoped).

## After the boot: the high-risk window

The first weeks out of the boot feel like freedom. They are also a second risk window.

Useful walking advice many clinics use:

- Start with a **heel lift** (often around 1 cm) in both shoes
- Take **shorter steps**
- Use partial support or the boot for longer walks if you limp
- Do not force a “normal” gait if you are compensating

Limping with a flat foot and long strides is not bravery — it is a common way to stretch a still-malleable tendon.

## Strength milestones that actually mean something

Time alone does not clear you. Strength does.

| Stage | Typical milestone | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early post-boot rebuild | **15** quality single-leg heel raises | Enough capacity to progress walking and basic rehab |
| Later strength / sport prep | **25+** high-quality single-leg heel raises | Gold-standard endurance target before higher sport demand |
| Side-to-side comparison | Aim toward **~85–90%** of the other leg | Reduces compensation under fatigue |
| Power / sport | Hopping, jumping, controlled landings | Needed before cutting and competition |

Quality beats counting. Twenty-five shallow, cheated reps are not the same as twenty-five strong raises with good heel height and control.

Practical exercise ideas are covered in [7 best physical therapy exercises for Achilles rupture recovery](/articles/7-best-physical-therapy-exercises-for-achilles-rupture-recovery).

## Stretching: what not to do

It is important to **not** do vigorous calf stretches early. A healing Achilles that is pulled into dorsiflexion under stretch can elongate.

Most people regain useful flexibility through **gradual loaded movement** over months — not aggressive towel stretches in week 12. Tightness often settles naturally toward **12–18 months**. If something feels stuck, ask your physio for controlled progressions rather than forcing range.

## Return to sport: criteria, not calendar

Passing six months on a calendar does not mean you are ready for sprinting or cutting.

A practical pathway looks like:

1. Pain-free walking with a normal gait
2. Strong calf progression (toward 25+ single-leg heel raises)
3. Easy jogging on flat ground, then volume before speed
4. Low-level hopping and landing control
5. Sport-specific drills, then modified training, then full competition

Many patients need around **9 months** before full sport feels realistic. Some elite pathways aim earlier; some need longer. The decision should be **criteria-based**: strength symmetry, hopping quality, pain-free loading, and confidence — not a date alone.

## What this means for your recovery

Successful **Achilles rupture rehab** is a long game with a simple theme: **protect length, build strength, progress by ability**.

Use this checklist with your clinician:

| Ask yourself | Good sign |
| --- | --- |
| Am I following boot / wedge / night rules? | Position protected as prescribed |
| Am I loading in tip-toe under guidance? | Progressive seated → standing calf work |
| Can I walk without a limp after boot removal? | Heel lift and short steps if needed |
| How are my heel raises? | Building toward 15, then 25+ quality reps |
| Am I stretching hard into dorsiflexion? | No — wait for supervised, gradual progress |
| Ready for sport? | Criteria met, not just months completed |

Seek **urgent review** if you feel a new pop, sudden sharp Achilles pain, a new gap in the tendon, or sudden loss of push-off. Whole-leg swelling, calf pain with breathlessness, or chest pain also need urgent assessment because of clot risk.

Fear of another rupture is normal. Keep the other leg strong too — contralateral injury can happen later, even if it is uncommon.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long is Achilles rupture rehabilitation?

Most people are in a boot for about **10–12 weeks**, then rebuild strength for many more months. Useful sport readiness is often around **9 months**, and full confidence can take **12–18 months**. Your timeline depends on criteria, not only dates.

### When should I start physiotherapy after Achilles rupture?

Often from roughly **weeks 3–6**, and sometimes with guided loading even earlier in the boot. Follow your own team — starting too aggressively without position control is risky; starting too late can leave the calf very weak.

### Is it normal that my heel raises are still weak after the boot?

Yes. Boot-off means the tendon is ready for a new phase of loading, not that you are recovered. Many patients cannot yet do strong single-leg heel raises at that point. That is exactly what the next stage rebuilds.

### Should I stretch my Achilles after rupture?

Not aggressively early. Forced dorsiflexion stretches can contribute to elongation. Regain range gradually with your physio through controlled loading over months.

### Can I return to sport at 6 months after Achilles rupture?

Some people start higher-level preparation around then, but many are still rebuilding. Full sport usually needs objective strength, hopping control, and clinician clearance — often closer to **9 months** or later.
