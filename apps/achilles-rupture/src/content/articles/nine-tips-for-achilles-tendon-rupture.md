**Achilles tendon rupture recovery** is long, awkward, and full of small traps. These nine tips come from real clinic advice — the practical stuff that prevents re-injury, clots, falls, and miserable nights while your tendon heals.

They will not replace your hospital protocol. They will make following it easier.

## Key takeaways

- Ask about **blood thinners** — clot risk stays elevated for weeks while you are immobilised.
- Your Achilles needs **24/7 tip-toe protection**, including overnight.
- Keep **crutches beside the bed** so you never hop unprotected to the toilet.
- A **shower chair** and waterproof cover prevent falls and wet boots.
- Do your **physio homework** daily — clinic visits alone are not enough.
- After the boot, pick **stable shoes** and use **heel lifts** as advised.
- Soft **socks** beat sweaty liner-only skin.
- Keep the **other calf strong** — the opposite Achilles is also at risk later.

## 1. Take blood thinners seriously

An Achilles rupture plus reduced mobility raises the risk of a **blood clot in the leg** (deep vein thrombosis, or DVT) and, rarely, a clot travelling to the lungs.

Discuss prophylaxis with your team early. Many patients need tablets or injections for about **4–6 weeks** — roughly through the full tip-toe (equinus) phase — though exact duration varies. Oral tablets are often easier for longer courses than daily injections.

Warning signs that need urgent review:

- Swelling of the **whole leg** up to the knee (even if painless)
- New calf pain different from your rupture pain
- Chest pain, breathlessness, or coughing blood

More detail: [blood thinners after Achilles rupture](/articles/blood-thinners-after-achilles-rupture-recovery).

## 2. Protect your Achilles while you sleep

If you take the boot off at night and the ankle bends upward, the repairing tendon can tear again. Recovery is like Snakes and Ladders — one unprotected night can send you back to the start.

You must sleep with the foot held **toes-down**. That usually means:

- Sleeping in the **walking boot**, or
- Switching to a clinician-approved **night splint** once allowed (often from boot fitting for non-surgical care, or after early wound healing if you had surgery)

Boots are hot, heavy, and hard to sleep in. A purpose-built night splint is lighter for many people — still follow your own clinician’s rules.

## 3. Keep crutches by the bed

Night toilet trips are when people hop on the injured side “just for a second.” That second can re-rupture the tendon or force the ankle into dorsiflexion.

Put crutches (or your frame) **within arm’s reach of the bed** every night — cast or boot. Turn on a soft light. Do not hop.

## 4. Learn the upside-down crutch trick

When you sit on the toilet or a chair, ordinary crutches often slide away. Turn a crutch **upside down** and use the handgrip as a short, stable upright support while you lower or stand — a common physio tip for bathroom transfers.

Practice in daylight first. If balance feels unsafe, ask for a home assessment or occupational therapy advice.

## 5. Put a chair in the shower

Standing on one leg on wet tiles is a fall waiting to happen.

- Use a **shower chair or stool**
- Keep a waterproof boot cover if you shower with the boot on
- Non-slip mat on the floor
- Keep soap and shampoo within easy reach so you are not twisting

Wet boots stay smelly and skin softens underneath — both raise blister and fungal risk. Dry the limb and change socks after washing.

## 6. Take your physio home

Clinic sessions set the plan. **Daily homework** builds the calf.

Write down:

- Which exercises
- How many sets and reps
- What pain is normal vs stop-and-call
- When wedges change and when you may remove the boot for washing

If you only exercise on appointment days, progress stalls. Film yourself (with consent from your physio) so technique stays honest. For exercise ideas later in recovery, see [7 best physical therapy exercises](/articles/7-best-physical-therapy-exercises-for-achilles-rupture-recovery).

## 7. Choose your shoes wisely

When you leave the boot, do not jump straight into floppy flip-flops or worn-flat trainers.

Useful early choices:

- Supportive trainers with a stable heel
- **Heel lifts** in both shoes initially (often about 0.5–1 cm) as advised
- An **EVENup** or similar leveler on the uninjured shoe while you still wear the boot daytime, to reduce back and hip strain

Avoid aggressive stretching into dorsiflexion to “get the shoe on.” Tightness usually eases over months — forced stretching can elongate the healing tendon.

## 8. Use socks — not boot liners alone

Boot liners wick poorly for many people. Skin stays damp, then blisters, heat rash, or athlete’s foot follow.

Wear **clean moisture-wicking socks** (merino or technical sports socks work well) under or instead of relying on the liner alone, depending on your boot design. Change them daily. Keep antifungal cream available if you are prone to athlete’s foot.

## 9. Look after the other leg

While one Achilles heals, the other side does extra work — stairs, hopping transfers, uneven loading. Opposite-limb rupture risk is real later on (often quoted around **~5%** over time).

Keep the uninjured calf strong with simple work your physio clears:

- Single-leg balance
- Calf raises on the good side
- Hip and core strength so you do not twist through the back

Do not ignore new pain on the “good” side. Early niggles deserve a check, not bravado.

## What this means for your recovery

Big medical decisions matter — surgery vs boot, wedge schedule, physio milestones. These nine tips are the daily layer that keeps those decisions from failing at 2 a.m. on the way to the bathroom.

Prioritise in week one:

1. Confirm **blood thinner** plan
2. Set up **night protection**
3. Stage the house: crutches by bed, chair in shower, clear paths

Seek urgent help for a new pop, sudden loss of push-off, sharp new Achilles pain, whole-leg swelling, breathlessness, or chest pain.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most important tip after Achilles rupture?

**24/7 protection of foot position** — including sleep — plus clot prevention. Most catastrophic early setbacks come from unprotected dorsiflexion or ignored clot warning signs.

### How long do I need blood thinners after Achilles rupture?

Often about **4–6 weeks**, through the full equinus phase, but your team decides based on your risk. Do not stop early without advice.

### Can I sleep without the boot if I am careful?

No. You cannot control your ankle while asleep. Use the boot or an approved night splint.

### When can I stop using crutches?

When you can walk safely in the boot without limping into risk, as your clinician advises. Many people still keep crutches for night trips and longer distances early on.

### Why strengthen the uninjured leg?

It carries extra load now, and the opposite Achilles has a meaningful later rupture risk. Strong hips and the good calf make everyday life safer.
