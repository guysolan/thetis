### Clinical claims evaluated

The following claims are assessed (and limited) by this evaluation:

1. The device maintains the ankle in plantarflexion suitable for night-time / rest protection during early ATR recovery.  
2. When used as intended, the device has an acceptable clinical safety profile compared with the clinical alternative of sleeping in a walking boot (or removing the boot at night).  
3. The device is usable for sleep and rest and can improve the recovery experience versus sleeping in a walking boot.

Claims **not** made or evaluated as proven: superiority for re-rupture rate versus boot in a randomised controlled trial; treatment of tendinopathy or plantar fasciitis.

### Clinical background and state of the art

Complete Achilles tendon rupture is managed operatively or non-operatively. In both pathways, early protection typically requires the ankle to be held in equinus / plantarflexion to limit tendon tension and reduce the risk of re-rupture or elongation. Daytime care commonly uses a walking boot with wedges or an adjustable ATR boot.

Night-time care is a recognised practical problem: boots are heavy, hot and bulky, and many patients sleep poorly, loosen straps, or remove the boot — losing protection when unsupervised. “Night splints” already on many markets (including reimbursed German PG 23 Fußlagerungsorthesen) are typically designed for plantar fasciitis and hold the foot in **dorsiflexion**, which is the wrong direction for acute ATR.

There is therefore a clinical need for a lightweight orthosis that maintains **plantarflexion** specifically for ATR rest and sleep, used **in parallel with** a daytime walking boot — not as a replacement for weight-bearing immobilisation.

### Appraisal of clinical data

| Ref | Source | Type | Key findings (as presented) | Appraisal / limitations |
| ----- | -------- | ------ | ----------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| A | Manufacturer preclinical / usability sleep testing (Technical Documentation §6.6; n=71 uninjured users) | Preclinical / usability | Users slept in the device; no major safety incidents; design refined (strap elasticity, heel slit) to reduce overtightening and heel discomfort | Not ATR patients; supports wearability and design maturity, not clinical outcome endpoints |
| B | Little Z et al. — *Mother Knows Best — Improving recovery experience after Achilles tendon rupture* (BOFAS 2023) | Conference clinical series / experience | High patient satisfaction; no adverse outcomes reported versus traditional night boot wear in that series | Conference presentation — not a full peer-reviewed RCT; supports experience and tolerability |
| C | Peacock C, Solan M, Gaukroger A, George N — *Sleeping safe and sound after Achilles rupture* (BOFAS 2024) | Conference observational / safety analysis | No difference in complication rates or functional outcomes versus control in the presented analysis; authors reported 95% CI for night injury risk with the splint &lt;0.1% | Conference data; strengthens safety narrative for intended night use; not a pivotal efficacy RCT |
| D | Solan M et al. — ATR multidisciplinary QIP, Royal Surrey (EFORT 2023) | Quality improvement / pathway | Splint pathway associated with shorter time to definitive plan and faster A&E→specialist review versus prior plaster-based route | Pathway efficiency benefit; secondary to core night-immobilisation claim |
| E | Thetis patient sleep survey (n=195) | Survey — care gap | 77% struggled to sleep in a walking boot; 26% removed the boot completely; 35% removed or adjusted the boot | Documents clinical need; not a device outcome study |
| F | Post-market surveillance / field use | PMS | 5,000+ devices in use; no formal medical-device vigilance complaints; no product recalls; no re-rupture/injury events attributed to the device in manufacturer PMS framing to date | Real-world safety signal monitoring; depends on reporting completeness; Amazon retail dissatisfaction treated as commercial feedback unless safety-related |

Evidence PDFs for B–D are held in the technical file / evidence annex.

### Analysis and conclusions

Taking the state of the art and the data above together:

- **Performance:** The device principle (rigid dorsal shell holding an obtuse / plantarflexed ankle angle, secured by straps) is consistent with established ATR immobilisation aims. Usability testing and clinical pathway/experience data support that the device can be worn for sleep/rest as intended.  
- **Safety:** Available conference safety analysis and PMS to date do not identify an unacceptable residual risk for intended use. Residual risks (e.g. misuse if walked in; strap pressure) are mitigated by design and IFU warnings and remain acceptable versus the alternative of unprotected or poorly tolerated boot sleep.  
- **Clinical benefit:** The principal benefit is maintained plantarflexion protection at night without requiring the patient to sleep in a walking boot, addressing a documented compliance and comfort gap. Secondary benefit: use in acute pathways as an alternative to plaster while awaiting definitive boot/plan.

**Benefit–risk determination:** For the intended purpose, indication and patient population, the clinical benefits outweigh the residual risks. The clinical evidence is sufficient to support CE marking as a Class I device. Evidence is proportionate to risk class; a large randomised efficacy trial is not required to demonstrate the intended immobilisation/function of this orthosis, but continuous PMS (and PMCF activities in §6.7.4) remain necessary.

**Equivalence:** No equivalent purpose-built ATR plantarflexion night splint is identified as a predicate for equivalence. Clinical comparison is made to the standard alternative of night-time walking-boot wear (or boot removal). Plantar fasciitis night splints are not equivalent (opposite ankle position / different indication).

## 6.7.3 Clinical investigations

No clinical investigation under MDR Article 62 (CIP / ethics / competent authority dossier) has been performed specifically for CE conformity of this Class I device.

**Justification for non-performance of a dedicated clinical investigation**

- The device is Class I, non-invasive, and applies a well-understood immobilisation principle (maintenance of plantarflexion).  
- Clinical evidence sufficient for the risk class is available from usability/preclinical testing, clinical pathway and conference observational series, and post-market experience.  
- A prospective randomised investigation is not considered necessary to demonstrate the basic intended performance of holding plantarflexion for night/rest use.

Where NHS / conference work was conducted under local audit or institutional arrangements, those outputs are cited in §6.7.1 as clinical data; they are not presented here as MDR Art. 62 investigations.
